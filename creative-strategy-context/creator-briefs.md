# Creator briefs

A creator brief is the direction handed to the person who'll actually film the ad. The whole craft of it is one tension: how much to lock down versus how much to leave open. Get that call right and the creator does their best work; get it wrong and you either kill the spark with over-scripting or get something off-brand from too little guidance.

This is the brief-*writing* layer. The upstream reasoning — what an idea, a concept, and a brief each are, and the rule that unscripted moments need open-ended briefs — lives in `ideation-and-brainstorming.md`; read it first. This doc is about how to actually shape the brief once you know what the concept is.

## Freedom vs. direction is a multi-variable call

There is no one-size-fits-all brief. How much freedom to give is a decision you make per situation, weighing the creator, the concept, and the format. Two poles to place a brief between:

- **Freestyle brief** — talking points, a reference, a direction, and room for the creator to bring their own spin. Right when the power of the ad is the creator's natural, unscripted reaction.
- **Director's brief** — heavily scripted, closer to a shot-by-shot. Right when the concept depends on precise beats, claims, or timing.

The move that decides most briefs:

- **Authorities do better with more freedom.** A credible expert or a strong personality performs worse on a tight script and better with room to talk in their own voice. The more the ad rides on who the person is, the looser the brief should be.

## The talent gate — freedom is earned, not default

Freedom only works with creators who can carry it. The working ratio (Alex, Aug 2025 what's-working conversation): **the bottom 90% of creators do better with a script; the top 10% do better without one.** Handing freedom to a weak creator produces something worse than the script would have — so the freestyle brief is a privilege you extend after you've seen what a creator does with rope, not the default.

Two more gates on top of raw talent:

- **The creator has to know the problem.** Someone who genuinely has the pain point — or has lived the transformation — can riff authentically; someone who doesn't understand the problem will miss no matter how good the brief is (Barry Hott, April 2026). The exception is a genuinely skilled actor teed up with the right script, which is a different brief on the director's end of the spectrum.
- **Delivery is a variable as big as the script.** Same exact script, two creators, both launched: one scaled past $15k/day with a CPA about $20 cheaper at scale, the other faded (Harry Delmege, March 2026, stated from his accounts). Model selection is among the most underrated calls in the whole process — a great story delivered by someone who can't yap comes out as a bad ad.

And a category gate that overrides creator quality:

- **Regulated categories almost never get freedom.** Supplements and anything claims-sensitive: a riffing creator will say something you can't legally run ("this gave me so much energy"). Script it.
- **Experience products almost never get a full script.** Food, taste tests, genuine reactions: the reaction is the ad, and a scripted reaction reads as one. Structure it, don't script it.

## What makes a brief land

- **Clear references help massively.** A reference video does more than a paragraph of description — it shows the tone of voice, the setup, the pacing, the energy you're after. Lead with references wherever you can.
- **Give a story structure.** Even a freestyle brief should hand the creator a shape to build on — the arc the content should follow — rather than leaving them to invent structure from scratch. Direction on *structure* and freedom on *delivery* is often the right split.
- **If you script one thing, script the hook.** The working shape of Adcrate's freestyle brief (shown in the March 2026 ugly-ads masterclass): an overall instruction for the video, a recommended structure, talking points and value props, must-get shots, reference links, and "make it your own" — with the hook written out word-for-word. The hook is where precision pays and the body is where scripting kills; a fully scripted yapper ad comes out like every other UGC ad.
- **Be exact about the first frame.** Creators won't get the shot unless the brief spells it out: phone propped against the kitchen counter is a different ad than a tripod, which is a different ad than a fisheye in the corner. The first frame decides the scroll (see `killer-performance-ads.md`, Ugly Ads), so the visual direction for the opening shot is the one place a freestyle brief should read like a director's brief.
- **Even a full script gets an authenticity valve.** On the scripted end, the instruction that keeps performance (Lucas Hamby / Ollie Hudson, Aug 2025): keep the framework exactly as written, but change any line that doesn't sound like you. The structure is load-bearing; the wording is the creator's.
- **Speak the creator's language.** Briefs work better when they use the shared vocabulary creators already film to. Parker should carry and use a running list of these terms, for example:
  - **POV** — first-person camera, as if the viewer is living the moment.
  - **Handheld selfie** — creator holding the camera at arm's length, talking to it.
  - **Direct-to-camera** — creator speaking straight into the lens.
  - **B-roll** — supplementary footage cut over the talking (the product, the hands, the setting).
  - (This vocabulary grows — add terms as briefs use them, and pair each with a plain description so a creator reads it the same way you meant it.)

## Don't over-systemize briefing

The operational temptation — especially at agency scale — is one template, every creator, every brief. It's simpler and it produces more volume, and at scale it makes all the ads come out the same even when the angles and scripts differ, because one briefing system is one lens. The rule that beats the template (Alex, Aug 2025): the brief's only job is to transmit the vision in your head to the person filming. Fully scripted, talking points, or a blank document — whatever carries that specific vision for that specific creator is the right format.

## Sourcing and keeping the creators worth briefing loosely

The freestyle end of this doc only works if the roster has people who can yap. They are rare, and the sourcing is its own discipline (March 2026 masterclass; corroborated across the 2025-2026 interviews):

- **Hunt where talking sells.** TikTok Shop creators get paid only when their content converts, so the top ones in your niche are pre-validated. Comedians and anyone who can hold you for two minutes on camera are candidates even if they've never sold anything — the skill is holding attention while feeling unstaged.
- **Track people down.** When an ad, an organic post, or a yapper surfaces, find the person — DM, email, whatever it takes. Adcrate keeps a person dedicated to finding and contacting creators; the habit matters more than the org chart.
- **Keep them once found.** Long-term deals, retainers, and performance incentives (a percentage of spend works for raw story formats). A creator who can riff authentically on your problem will make the account real money for a long time, and losing one costs more than the retainer.
