# Process — Make the Invisible Visible

Use an image model to render an **intentionally unrealistic visual** that shows what a customer feels. Symptoms and sensations are invisible, and customers describe them vividly. Rendering the description literally stops the person who has that feeling.

This is the most practical use of AI image generation for statics, and it depends entirely on having the customer's language first.

## When to pick

- **Best for:** problem/solution brands where the problem is a sensation nobody can photograph — nerve pain, bloating, itch, fatigue, brain fog, pressure. Also emotional states with a physical metaphor attached.
- **Worst for:** lifestyle brands where the appeal is aspiration rather than relief, and any product whose benefit is already visible. If you can photograph it, photograph it.

## The move

Pull the sensations from reviews and comments, then ask what would make each one visible.

The worked example: a customer writes that their foot feels like it has needles in it. The ad renders that — needles in the foot, unmistakably unreal. Anyone with that sensation stops, because the ad is showing them the inside of their own experience, which no product photo can do.

The generative model is what makes this cheap. Before, this required a concept shoot; now it requires the right sentence and a prompt.

## Execution

1. **Mine the sensation language first.** Search reviews and comments for how customers describe the feeling — the similes especially. "Feels like," "it's as if," "like someone is." Similes are pre-built visual briefs.
2. **Do not invent the sensation.** A surreal visual with no customer language behind it is surrealism for its own sake, and it will not resonate because nobody described it that way. The language is the evidence the image will land.
3. **Pick the most literal reading.** The power is in rendering the description exactly, not in interpreting it artfully. Needles in the foot, not an abstract representation of discomfort.
4. **Decide whether it shows the problem or the relief.** Problem-state visuals stop the sufferer; relief-state visuals sell the outcome. The problem state usually hooks harder on cold traffic.
5. **Pair it with the customer's line.** The visual and the quote reinforce each other — the image stops them, the words confirm the ad understands.
6. **Check compliance carefully.** Rendering a symptom can imply a medical claim. Depicting the problem is usually safer than depicting a cure; a visual promising resolution may need the same rails as a written claim.
7. **Hand the visual to production** with the specificity `ai-static-ad-generation.md` requires. Whatever the prompt leaves unspecified, the model invents.

## Output content

- The sensation, quoted from the source review or comment, dated.
- The visual, described precisely enough to prompt from.
- Whether it shows the problem or the relief, and why.
- The paired copy.
- The compliance read on depicting it.

## What never to do

- Generate a surreal visual with no customer language behind it.
- Interpret the description artfully instead of rendering it literally.
- Depict a cure or resolution without checking the claims rails.
- Use it for a product whose benefit could simply be photographed.
- Let the image be the whole ad. It stops the scroll; the copy still has to do the work.
