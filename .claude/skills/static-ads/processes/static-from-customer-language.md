# Process — Static From Customer Language

A customer's own words become the ad. This is the default idea path for any brand with a review corpus, and the one that most reliably breaks through a feed where every brand makes the same polished claim.

The principle: a customer talking to a customer outperforms a business talking to a customer. The reason it works is the specificity and imperfection of real speech, which is exactly what gets destroyed when someone "cleans it up."

## When to pick

- **Best for:** brands with vivid reviews or an active comment section. Apparel, supplements, anything where customers describe a felt result or a before-state they were stuck in.
- **Worst for:** thin or generic review corpora ("love it," "great product"), and compliance-heavy categories where customer claims trip the rails.

## What qualifies as a golden nugget

Follow `customer-review-mining-method.md`. In short, the line must be specific and visual, in the customer's own register, carrying an emotional charge — and it must not be a testimonial-trap phrase. "Best ever," "so good," "worth it" are filler, not nuggets.

The strongest nuggets name the **before state** — the resignation, the workaround, the thing they had given up on. Those are what a stranger in the same state recognizes instantly.

## Execution

1. **Mine, do not sample.** Search reviews and ad comments for the emotional register you need — resignation, relief, shame, surprise — rather than reading the most recent page. Search comments sorted by engagement too; a comment with hundreds of likes is a line the audience already voted on.
2. **Take it as written.** Do not paraphrase. Do not fix the grammar. Do not compress it into brand voice. If a line is worth using it is worth using intact.
3. **Decide the format from the line.** A long, vivid confession wants a testimonial layout. A short punchy phrase can carry a headline-led static.
4. **Handle length by format.** This is where this process diverges from the `headlines` skill's verbatim-lift process, deliberately:
   - **Headline-led formats** inherit the under-ten-words cap. Trim only what blocks comprehension.
   - **Verbatim quote formats — testimonial, comment-response, review-screenshot — are exempt.** Carry the customer's sentence at the customer's length. Trimming a fourteen-word quote to satisfy a word cap *is* paraphrasing it. Let the design carry the length: the quote gets the size, the attribution gets a line beneath it.
5. **Run the two governors.** The claims governor — a customer may say things the brand legally cannot, and the rails hold regardless of who said it. The voice governor — the line must still sound like this brand's world.
6. **Run the stranger test.** Does the line land on someone who has never heard of the brand? A nugget that only makes sense to an existing customer is a bottom-funnel asset, not an acquisition static.
7. **Attribute it.** Reviewer first name, star rating, date. Real attribution is part of why it reads as real.

## Output content

- The exact copy as it will appear on the ad, including attribution.
- The full source review or comment, quoted, with its date and rating. Quote the whole thing, not just the extracted line — the reader should see what it was pulled from.
- Which trim, if any, and why.
- The claim and voice checks it passed.
- The persona and emotional state it targets.

## What never to do

- Paraphrase. This is the one rule in this process that has no exception.
- Invent a "customer-sounding" line. If no review said it, it is fabrication in a customer costume.
- Polish grammar until it sounds like marketing. The roughness is the asset.
- Trim a verbatim quote to fit a headline word cap.
- Run a line that breaks compliance because a customer said it.
- Use a testimonial-trap phrase as the hook.
