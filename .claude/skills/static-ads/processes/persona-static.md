# Process — Persona Static

Build the whole ad for one specific person. Not "our audience" — one person, with a situation specific enough that everyone else scrolls past and that person stops.

The traditional instruction was to make ads relevant to as many people as possible. In a feed that is already hyper-relevant to every individual, the sharper move is often the opposite. Excluding the other personas is not a cost, because **you can make ads for them too** — that is the point of the approach, not a limitation of it.

## When to pick

- **Best for:** accounts where the white-space read surfaced a persona or demographic gap, brands with several distinct buyer types, and categories where the buying reason differs sharply by segment.
- **Worst for:** single-persona brands with genuinely one buyer, and brands with no persona evidence — a persona invented for an ad is a guess wearing research clothes.

## Two levels of directness

- **Named.** The ad says who it is for outright: "If you run a real estate brokerage doing a million a year," "We're looking for girlies that feel puffy for no reason." Maximum qualification, maximum exclusion. Strongest when the persona is an identifiable role or situation the person would use to describe themselves.
- **Shown.** The ad depicts the person and the situation, and recognition does the qualifying. Someone who sees a body like theirs, in a room like theirs, registers "this is for me" without being told. Strongest for demographic and body-type personas, where naming would be clumsy or insulting.

Pick by whether the persona is something people call themselves. "Bodybuilder with a small waist and big thighs" is shown, not named.

## Execution

1. **Take the persona from evidence, not invention.** The brand's persona docs, post-purchase surveys, and the review corpus. When the persona came from the demographic gap in the white-space read, verify it in the reviews — find the people in that cohort writing about the product in their own words.
2. **Name the specific situation, not the segment.** "Men 55+" is a media buy, not a persona. "The guy who quietly stopped wearing jeans years ago and defaulted to sweatpants" is a persona, and it is the reviews that give you that.
3. **Pull that persona's language.** Search the corpus filtered to their vocabulary and their concerns. They talk differently from the rest of the base, and the difference is the whole asset.
4. **Write the message only for them.** Every line. No hedging toward broader relevance — hedging is what makes the ad generic again.
5. **Match the visual to the persona.** This is where persona statics most often fail: the copy is built for a 60-year-old and the frame shows a 25-year-old. If the brand has no shot of this person, that is a production dependency and it must be flagged, not designed around.
6. **Note the funnel implication.** The strongest version of this continues past the click — a landing page written for the same person. Larger brands run a full funnel per persona. Flag the opportunity even when this run only produces the static.

## Output content

- The persona, as a situation rather than a demographic bracket.
- The evidence behind it: reviews, survey responses, or the demographic read, quoted and counted.
- Named or shown, and why.
- The copy, entirely in their register.
- The visual, with any out-of-play requirement flagged plainly.
- The funnel note where it applies.

## What never to do

- Invent a persona to justify a creative idea.
- Write a persona static that hedges toward everyone else.
- Use a demographic bracket as a persona.
- Build the copy for one person and the visual for another.
- Name a persona in a way the person would find insulting rather than recognizing.
