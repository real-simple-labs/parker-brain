# Avatar matching: picking customer lines for this exact person

A great customer line from the wrong person is a weaker anchor than a good line from the right one. The check picks swap anchors by how closely the person who said them matches the avatar the ad is built for.

## The avatar, in fields

Fill what the concept and the brand context give you. Don't invent the rest; leave it blank.

- **Buyer.** Who pays. Use the brand's persona name where one fits.
- **Speaker.** Who talks in the ad. Often the buyer, but not always: a recipient can narrate a gift ad, a partner can narrate a couples ad.
- **Recipient / user.** Who the product is for, if not the buyer.
- **Moment.** The trigger: an occasion, a life change, a problem showing up, a season.
- **Life stage and context.** Only what the concept or the persona doc states (empty nester, new parent, recently moved, retired). Never inferred from a name.
- **The viewer.** Who's meant to see themselves in the ad. Usually the buyer.

## Reading who's speaking in a customer record

Use only what the record shows.

- **Role words in the text.** "I got this for my daughter" (giver), "my mom gave me this" (recipient), "I bought it for myself" (self-buyer), "we" (a couple or family).
- **Life-stage words in the text.** "since retiring," "now that the kids are grown," "we just moved here," "our first Christmas married."
- **Structured fields the source supplies.** Survey age band, purchase type (gift / self), segment, verified-buyer flag. Check how much of the data has the field filled in before leaning on it.
- **Never** a reviewer's name, profile photo, or handle as evidence of age, gender, or role.

## The three match levels

**Strong.** Same role as the speaker, same or adjacent moment. A mom buying for her grown kids, quoted for an ad where a mom buying for her grown kids is talking.

**Partial.** Same role, different moment; or same moment, adjacent role (a grandparent's line in a parent's ad). Usable, with a note on what doesn't line up.

**Cross-voice.** A different role entirely, but the line is too good to leave. Most often the recipient's reaction in a giver's ad, or a self-buyer's discovery line in a gift ad. A cross-voice line can anchor a swap only if the swap keeps the ad's speaker and doesn't make them claim the other person's experience. In the full read, a cross-voice line is shown where it can be attributed: an on-screen review card, an overlay quoting "a customer," primary text, a comment reply. On-screen attribution only works for public records (reviews and comments); a survey answer is private.

## Picking the anchor for a swap

When more than one customer line could anchor a swap, pick by:
1. Avatar match.
2. Signal strength: a metaphor, a specific number, a used-to/now contrast, alliteration, word-of-mouth, or a punchy line.
3. Recency.
4. Recurrence: a phrase three or more independent customers use beats a one-off.

## When the avatar isn't visible in the data

Sometimes the data can't separate the avatar: no age field, no role words in most records, or an audience the brand has never sold to. Say so in the line under the percentage, and pick anchors on meaning alone.
